Piano Pieces for a Small Keyboard (25, 37 and 49 Keys)

A full piano has 88 keys. The keyboard most people actually start on has 25, 37, 49 or 61, because that is what fits on a desk and costs what a beginner is willing to spend before knowing whether they will stick with it.

The usual advice — "get 88 weighted keys" — is true and useless. You have the keyboard you have. The real question is which music will physically play on it, and that question has an exact answer, so we measured it.

Every one of the 2,433 transcriptions in the library was parsed and its total pitch span recorded: the distance from its lowest note to its highest. If that span fits inside your keyboard, the piece fits. Here are the three tiers.

25 keys — 34 pieces

Two octaves. A 25-key controller is really a melody instrument, and the list reflects that: nursery tunes, carols, and the medieval Cantigas melody, plus a handful of short pieces that never stray far from where they start.

37 keys — 302 pieces

Three octaves, and the picture changes completely. At 37 keys you can reach most single-line song repertoire and a great deal of short character writing. This is the tier where the collection starts feeling like a library rather than a starter pack.

49 keys — 1,068 pieces

Four octaves, and 44% of the entire library is now available to you. This is the number worth knowing before you buy anything: a 49-key keyboard is not a toy, it is access to over a thousand pieces of real music.

What the numbers mean in practice

The jump from 25 to 37 keys multiplies your repertoire by nine. The jump from 37 to 49 multiplies it by three and a half again. After that the returns flatten out — going from 49 to 61 keys adds far less than the previous step did, because most of what remains beyond four octaves is music that also needs a pedal, a full hand span and years of practice.

So if you are buying: 49 keys is the point where the curve bends. Below it you are limited by the instrument. Above it you are limited by your hands, which is the right thing to be limited by.

An honest caveat about transposition

A piece that spans more than your keyboard is not always out of reach. Every piece on this site has a transpose control, and shifting a piece up or down by a few semitones moves the whole thing into a different part of the keyboard. If a piece is only slightly too low for your range, transposing it up will often bring it inside — it will sound higher than written, but it will play.

What transposing cannot do is narrow a piece. If the span itself is wider than your keyboard, no amount of shifting will fit it, because the distance between the lowest and highest note is fixed. That is why the lists above are built on span rather than on absolute pitch: span is the property that decides.

What else matters besides key count

Key size. Some very small keyboards use narrow keys. Learn on those and the muscle memory you build will be slightly wrong for a real piano. If you have a choice, full-size keys matter more than key count.

Touch. Unweighted plastic keys are fine for finding notes and building familiarity. They will not build the finger strength or the dynamic control a real piano needs. This is a real limitation, but it is not a reason to wait — starting on a light keyboard beats not starting.

Sustain pedal. More repertoire depends on the pedal than on the eighty-eighth key. Many cheap keyboards have a pedal socket even when they have 49 keys; a pedal costs very little and unlocks more than an extra octave would.

The honest summary

If you have 25 keys, you can play thirty-four real pieces today — that is a month of learning, not a dead end. If you have 37, three hundred. If you have 49, more than a thousand, and you will not exhaust that in years.

Nobody needs 88 keys to start. Everybody needs to start.
