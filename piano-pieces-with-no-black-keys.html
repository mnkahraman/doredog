Piano Pieces With No Black Keys (All 21 of Them)

Everyone who sits down at a piano for the first time notices the same thing: the white keys look approachable and the black ones look like they belong to somebody who knows what they are doing. That instinct is not wrong. The black keys are where sharps and flats live, and sharps and flats are where key signatures live, and key signatures are the part of written music that makes beginners close the lid.

So here is a question worth answering properly: how much real music can you play without ever touching a black key?

We checked. Every one of the 2,433 transcriptions in the library was parsed note by note, and 21 of them never use a single black key — not once, from the first note to the last. This is that list.

Why this list is shorter than you would hope

Twenty-one out of 2,433 is under one percent, and that number tells you something real about music rather than something about this library.

A piece that avoids black keys entirely is, in practice, a piece in C major or A minor that also never borrows a chord from anywhere else and never changes key. Composers change key constantly — it is one of the main ways music creates the feeling of going somewhere and coming back. The moment a piece modulates, even briefly, a black key appears. Add a single passing chromatic note for colour, and it appears. Write in almost any key other than C, and it appears immediately.

So the pieces that survive this filter tend to be one of three things: music written before the modern key system settled, music written deliberately for beginners, or folk melody that was never harmonised in the first place.

What is actually in here

The oldest item on the list is a Cantiga de Santa Maria, from the collection compiled at the court of Alfonso X of Castile — the surviving codices were put together in the 1270s and early 1280s, up to his death in 1284 (Wikipedia). It contains no black keys for a reason that has nothing to do with beginners: it predates the harmonic system that produces them. Medieval modal melody simply does not need the notes.

At the other end sit the nursery tunes and carols — Twinkle, Twinkle, Frère Jacques, Hot Cross Buns, Joy to the World, Jingle Bells. These are on the list because generations of people have sung them in whatever key was easy, and the version that survived into print is the version that sits comfortably on white keys.

Between those two extremes are the interesting ones. **Satie's Enfantillages pittoresques belongs to the third category and the second at once: written in October 1913 as one of three sets of beginner pieces, built deliberately around the five fingers of each hand, and intended — in Satie's characteristically sideways way — to prepare children for the sound of modern music rather than shelter them from it (Wikipedia). Two movements of a Dussek sonata are here, as is a Chausson mélodie, a Zumsteeg lied, and a Bantock** song setting a Chinese poem in translation that is, by our measurements, the hardest piece on the list at 77 out of 100.

That last one is the useful surprise. No black keys and easy are not the same thing. A piece can stay on white keys and still move fast, leap wide and stack thick chords.

How to use this list

If you are on your first week at a keyboard, start at the top. The pieces are ordered by measured difficulty, and the first dozen are genuinely playable in an afternoon.

If you are past that, use this list differently: as a way to feel what a key signature actually does. Play the Satie, then play something from the same era with four flats in it. The physical difference in your hands is the thing that written music is trying to tell you with those symbols at the start of the line. Letter notes skip the symbol and hand you the note — but the difference is still there under your fingers, and noticing it is worth more than memorising it.

One thing this list does not mean

It does not mean these pieces are in C major. Several are in A minor, which shares the same white keys, and the medieval items are in modes that predate major and minor altogether. If you go on to read standard notation, do not expect a blank key signature on all of them.

And it does not mean black keys are hard. They are narrower and set back, which is genuinely awkward at first, but they are also the landmarks that let a pianist find their way without looking — the pattern of twos and threes is how your hand knows where it is. Avoiding them forever would make playing harder, not easier.

Start here. Do not stay here.
