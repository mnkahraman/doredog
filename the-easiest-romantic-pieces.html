The Easiest Romantic Pieces, Measured

The Romantic era is where most people's favourite piano music lives, and it is also where most beginners get hurt. Chopin, Liszt, Rachmaninoff — the pieces that make you want to play the piano in the first place are frequently the ones written for people who had already been practising for fifteen years.

But the era is enormous. 1,532 of the 2,433 pieces in this library are Romantic — nearly two thirds — and inside that pile is a great deal of music written specifically for amateurs playing at home. We measured all of it. It runs from 3 out of 100 to 100, and here it is, easiest first.

Why the library leans so heavily Romantic

Two reasons, neither of them a curatorial choice.

Everything here is public domain, which draws a hard line across music history: the nineteenth century is fully open, the twentieth only partly. And the nineteenth century was the age of domestic music-making — the piano was the home entertainment system, and publishers printed enormous quantities of short, playable pieces and songs for people to buy and play in their own front rooms. Schubert alone wrote over six hundred songs.

That commercial fact is why a beginner's Romantic repertoire exists at all.

The top of the list is teaching music

Scroll the easiest twenty and a pattern is obvious: Schumann's Album for the Young, method-book openings, character pieces with titles like Innocence and Consolation. Twenty pieces in the whole Romantic collection score under 20 out of 100, and 150 score under 40.

That second number is the useful one. 150 genuinely approachable pieces is more than you will get through in a year, and it is drawn from the era whose sound most people actually want.

The trap: fame and difficulty are unrelated

This is the single most common mistake, and the Romantic era is where it does the most damage.

The famous pieces are famous because they are extraordinary, and quite often they are extraordinary because of what they ask of the player. The Revolutionary Étude measures 96. The third movement of the Moonlight Sonata measures 100. Wanting to play them is completely reasonable; starting with them is not.

The good news is that the same composers usually wrote something at the other end. Chopin's Op. 28 No. 7 is forty-five seconds long and genuinely playable. Schumann's Album for the Young is by the man who also wrote the Kreisleriana. Brahms's Lullaby is by the composer of the hardest song in this library.

Use the L1–L10 badge on every card rather than the reputation of the name.

What makes Romantic music harder than Baroque

Worth understanding, because it tells you what to practise.

The stretches. The piano grew an iron frame and a bigger sound, and composers wrote for hands spanning a tenth. Baroque keyboard music rarely asks for more than an octave.

The pedal. Much Romantic writing assumes the sustain pedal is holding the harmony while the hands move elsewhere. Without it the texture falls apart. This is the one piece of equipment that matters more than extra keys.

Rubato. The tempo is meant to stretch and pull rather than tick. Letter notes give you the notes and the order; the flexibility is yours to add, and it is the part that takes longest to learn.

Thickness. Six notes under two hands on a single beat is normal here and almost unknown in Bach.
