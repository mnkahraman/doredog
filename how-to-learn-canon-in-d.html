How to Learn Canon in D (Pachelbel) in Letter Notes

You've heard Canon in D a thousand times — at weddings, graduations, in films, and echoing through countless pop songs. The good news for a beginner is that it's built on a repeating pattern, which makes it one of the most learnable of all famous pieces. Here's what it is and how to start playing it.

What Canon in D really is

  • It is properly titled the Canon and Gigue in D major, by the German Baroque composer Johann Pachelbel (1653–1706). Its exact date is unknown (suggestions range from 1680 to 1706). (Wikipedia)
  • It is a true three‑part canon over a repeating ground bass — the same short bass line loops from beginning to end. (Wikipedia)
  • Remarkably, it sat in obscurity for centuries and only became famous after a 1968 recording; today it's a wedding‑ceremony staple, and its looping chord progression has shaped countless pop songs. (Wikipedia)

Why it's beginner‑friendly

The entire piece rests on the same eight‑note bass line and chord progression, repeated over and over. Learn that one pattern and you have the backbone of the whole piece — everything else is layered gradually on top. That repetition is exactly why Canon in D rewards patient beginners.

How to learn it on DoReDog

  1. Listen first. Play it through once so the shape is in your ear: Canon in D on DoReDog.
  2. Learn the repeating bass. In the left hand, the same short pattern loops the whole way through. In letter notes you just follow the letters — no key signature to decode. Get this loop comfortable first.
  3. Slow the tempo right down. Give yourself time to find each key.
  4. Add the melody in layers. The right‑hand part grows more elaborate as the piece goes on. Learn it a section at a time, over the bass you already know.
  5. Loop the tricky variation. Use the A–B loop to drill any single busy bar until it's automatic.
  6. Play it through slowly, daily. Because it repeats, once the pattern clicks the rest falls into place fast.

A tip that makes it click

Think of Canon in D as one repeating loop with a melody that slowly blossoms on top. You're never really learning a whole new page — just a little more decoration over the same familiar chords. That's the secret to why so many people can play it.
