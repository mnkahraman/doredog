The Slowest Piano Pieces in the Library

Beginners are usually pointed at easy music. What they often need is slow music, and those are different things.

Difficulty is about what your hands are asked to do — how far they travel, how many notes land at once, how many accidentals interrupt the pattern. Speed is about how much time you get to do it. A piece can be technically simple and still feel impossible because the notes arrive faster than you can find them. And a piece can look complicated on the page and be perfectly manageable because it gives you a full second between each one.

So we measured the second thing directly. Every transcription in the library was parsed and its note rate calculated — actual notes divided by actual seconds. Then we kept the ones that run at under two notes a second and sit within reach of a learner. There are 35 of them, sorted slowest first.

Two notes a second is slower than it sounds

Count it out: one, and, two, and. Two notes a second is one note on every count and one on every "and". At that rate you have half a second per note — enough time to look at the letter, find the key, and press it deliberately rather than by reflex.

For comparison, the median piece in this library runs at just under eight notes a second counting both hands, and 706 pieces pass ten. Those are not pieces you sight-find. They are pieces you have already memorised and are now executing.

The music below is the opposite. It is designed to be dwelt in.

Satie owns the top of this list

All three Gymnopédies land in the twelve slowest pieces we could find, which is not a coincidence — it is the entire point of them. Satie marked them, in order, Lent et douloureux, Lent et triste and Lent et grave: slow and painful, slow and sad, slow and grave. The first and third were published in 1888, the second not until 1895 (Wikipedia).

They are also the best argument on this site for the difference between slow and easy. Our measurements put Gymnopédie No. 1 at 42 out of 100 — the hardest of the three — because the left hand makes wide, deliberate leaps between a low bass note and a chord above it. Nothing is fast. Everything takes reach and control. A beginner can absolutely learn it, and will spend the time on accuracy rather than on speed, which is a far more pleasant way to spend it.

What else slows music down

Look at what shares the list with Satie and a pattern emerges.

Sacred and devotional music. Clari's Ave maris stella is near the top. Music written for worship moves at the speed of a congregation and a resonant room — fast music in a stone building turns to mud, so it was not written.

Carols and hymn tunes. O Little Town of Bethlehem, Away in a Manger. Same reason: these are melodies built for a crowd of untrained singers to follow, so every note is given room.

Teaching material. Bériot's Méthode de violon opens the list at just over one note per second. Method books start slow deliberately — the whole purpose is to give the student time.

Nursery melody. Twinkle, Twinkle, Mary Had a Little Lamb, Old MacDonald. Songs that survived by being singable by children move at a child's pace.

Music slows down for a reason, and the reason is almost always that somebody needed time — to sing it, to learn it, to hear it in a big room. You are now that somebody.

How to use slow pieces well

The temptation with slow music is to speed it up as soon as you can manage it. Resist that for longer than feels necessary.

Slow pieces expose everything. When notes are far apart you can hear whether they are actually even, whether the chord is truly together, whether the sound stops dead or carries. Fast music hides all of that under momentum. This is why teachers make people practise slowly — not because it is a stepping stone to fast, but because it is where the listening happens.

If a piece here still feels hurried, take the speed control down further. Nothing on this site has a floor at "as written". And if the reverse happens — the piece feels too still, and you catch yourself wanting to move — that is worth noticing too. It usually means you are ready for something with more going on.
