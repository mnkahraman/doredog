Hands-Separate Practice: Why It Works and How to Do It

If one piece of practice advice is nearly universal among piano teachers, it's this: when a passage is hard, practise each hand on its own before you put them together. It feels slower. It isn't. Here's why it works and exactly how to do it.

Why playing hands‑together is so hard at first

Your two hands are usually doing different jobs at the same time: the right hand carries the melody while the left plays a bass line or chords, often in a different rhythm. Coordinating both while also figuring out which keys to press is a lot to manage at once. When you try to learn everything simultaneously, you tend to learn all of it badly — hesitations, wrong notes and tension get baked in.

Splitting the hands lets you solve one problem at a time. Each hand gets to become confident and almost automatic on its own, so that when you combine them, you're coordinating two things you already know instead of learning three things at once.

How to practise hands separately (step by step)

  1. Right hand first. Play just the melody line, slowly, until you can do it without stopping to think. The melody is usually the part you can hear in your head, so your ear helps you check it.
  2. Left hand next. Now play only the left‑hand part, equally slowly. This hand often feels more foreign because you don't "sing" the bass — so give it real, separate attention.
  3. Both hands, but very slowly. Combine them at a tempo far below performance speed — slow enough that you never have to stop or guess. Speed is the reward for accuracy, not the goal.
  4. Isolate the seam. The hardest spot is usually where the hands have to line up in a tricky rhythm. Loop just that bar, hands together, until the coordination feels automatic.
  5. Raise the tempo gradually. Only speed up once a pass is clean. If mistakes creep back in, drop the tempo again. Clean‑and‑slow always beats fast‑and‑messy.

A few honest tips

  • Don't rush to combine. The urge to hear the "real" piece is strong, but a few extra minutes per hand saves a lot of un‑learning later.
  • Keep the tempo the same across both hands so they'll actually fit together when combined.
  • Short, frequent sessions win. Ten focused minutes today and ten tomorrow beat an exhausting hour once a week — your hands consolidate what they've learned between sessions.

Hands‑separate practice isn't a beginner's crutch that advanced players outgrow. Professionals use it on their hardest passages for their whole careers. It's simply how the piano is learned.
