How to Learn a Piano Piece by Ear, Step by Step

Playing "by ear" sounds like a gift some people are born with. It isn't — it's a skill, and it's one of the most useful a pianist can build. Learning a tune by listening trains your musical memory, your sense of pitch, and your ability to play with other people. Best of all, you can start with songs you already know. Here's a practical, no‑theory method.

Why learning by ear is worth it

When you learn by ear, you're not just memorising a piece — you're teaching your brain to connect the sound you hear with the key that makes it. Over time that connection becomes fast and automatic, until you can pick out melodies, work out chords, and even improvise. It's the difference between reading a language and actually speaking it.

Start with the right kind of tune

Choose a melody that is:

  • Familiar — one you can already hum without thinking. Your memory of how it "should" sound is your built‑in answer key.
  • Simple — a nursery tune, a folk song, or a slow theme, not a virtuoso showpiece.
  • Short — you can always add more once the first phrase clicks.

The step‑by‑step method

  1. Find the very first note. Hum the opening, then hunt on the keyboard until a key matches your voice. That single note is your anchor.
  2. Work in tiny phrases. Take just the first two or three notes. Sing them, then find them one at a time. Don't move on until they match what you hear.
  3. Go up or down by feel. Ask a simple question for each new note: does the tune go up, down, or stay? Then: by a little or a lot? Your ear already knows — you're just locating it.
  4. Repeat and extend. Play what you have, add the next couple of notes, play from the start again. The repetition glues the phrase into memory.
  5. Check yourself against the real thing. When you're stuck, listen to the piece again — or peek at the letter notes to confirm a tricky spot, then go back to using your ear.
  6. Add the left hand last. Once the melody is secure, find the bass notes underneath the same way: one at a time, by sound.

How DoReDog helps you train your ear

  • Hear the piece any time and slow it down, so fast passages become slow enough to pick out note by note.
  • Loop a short phrase with the A–B loop and listen to it as many times as you need.
  • Use the letter notes as a "check," not a crutch. Try a phrase by ear first; glance at the notes only to confirm. Over time you'll peek less and less.
  • Isolate one hand to hear exactly what the melody or bass is doing on its own.

A realistic expectation

Your first by‑ear tune will feel slow and fiddly. The tenth will feel natural. That's not talent arriving — that's the skill being built, exactly the way everyone builds it. Start with one simple melody you love, and let your ear lead.
