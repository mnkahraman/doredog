Letter Notes vs. Sheet Music: Which Should a Beginner Learn?

Both letter notes and sheet music answer the same two questions: which key do I press, and when? They just encode the answer differently. Neither is "better" in the abstract — they're suited to different goals and different moments in your journey. Here's an honest comparison so you can choose.

What sheet music gives you

Standard notation — notes on a five‑line staff — is the shared language of trained musicians. Its strengths are real:

  • Precision. It captures exact rhythms, subtle timing, dynamics (loud/soft), articulation and phrasing that simpler systems leave out.
  • Universality. Almost any published music, for any instrument, is available in it. Learn to read it and the entire written repertoire opens up.
  • Communication. You can play with other musicians, take lessons in a common language, and read a piece you've never heard.

The cost is time. Fluent staff‑reading takes sustained study before it pays off in songs you enjoy — which is exactly where many self‑taught beginners lose momentum.

What letter notes give you

Letter notes name each key with a letter and mark timing with spacing. Their strengths are the mirror image:

  • A near‑zero starting barrier. If you can read the alphabet, you can begin in minutes.
  • Momentum. You play real, recognisable music on day one, which keeps you coming back — and coming back is what actually builds skill.
  • A gentle on‑ramp to the keyboard. You learn where the notes live and how pieces are built, which makes everything — including sheet music later — easier.

The trade‑off is detail: letter notes carry melody, chords, both hands and basic timing, but not the fine expressive nuances standard notation records.

So which should you learn?

  • If you're a total beginner who mainly wants to play songs you love: start with letter notes. Build the habit, learn the keyboard, and enjoy playing. You can add staff reading later, from a much stronger position.
  • If your goal is to read any score, play in a group, or study formally: you'll want sheet music eventually — but there's no rule against using letter notes to get comfortable at the keys first.
  • The honest answer for most people: both, in order. Letter notes to fall in love with playing; sheet music later if and when your goals need it. Starting with the easy on‑ramp doesn't close the door on the detailed system — it makes that door easier to walk through.

The worst outcome isn't "choosing the wrong notation." It's quitting before you ever play. Letter notes exist to make sure that doesn't happen.
