Erik Satie for Beginners: Where to Start

If you're a beginner who wants to play something genuinely beautiful — not a simplified exercise — Erik Satie is the best composer to start with. His most famous pieces are slow, spacious and hypnotic, and that slowness works in your favour: a beginner playing them gently and carefully sounds right, not like someone struggling.

Why Satie suits beginners so well

Most famous classical music punishes you for playing slowly. Satie's does the opposite. His Gymnopédies are marked "slow and painful" — the unhurried pace is the actual style, not a compromise. There are no fast runs, no leaping hands, and the left hand is usually a calm repeating pattern you can learn once and reuse through the whole piece.

He was a French composer (1866–1925) who steered a new generation of French composers toward a leaner, sparer style. (Wikipedia)

Start here — the three Gymnopédies

Satie completed the three Gymnopédies in 1888. (Wikipedia)

  • Gymnopédie No. 1 — the famous one, and the best single starting point in the entire library. A gentle rocking left hand under a long, singable melody. (Full walkthrough: How to Play Gymnopédie No. 1)
  • Gymnopédie No. 2 — the same calm world, slightly warmer.
  • Gymnopédie No. 3 — rounds out the set. Learn one and the others come much faster, because the hand shapes repeat.

Next — the Gnossiennes

The Gnossiennes are stranger and more hypnotic. Satie wrote them without time signatures or bar lines — "free time" — and sprinkled the score with odd instructions like "with astonishment." He even invented the word gnossienne himself. (Wikipedia)

  • Gnossienne No. 2
  • Gnossienne No. 3

That missing time signature is actually freeing for a beginner: there's no rigid grid to fight. Let the phrases breathe.

Also worth knowing

Satie's short songs — the 4 Petites mélodies, including Adieu and Chanson — are brief, gentle and very approachable if you want something less familiar.

How to play Satie well

  1. Go slower than feels natural. With Satie, patience isn't a compromise — it's the style.
  2. Learn the left hand first. It's usually a repeating "bass‑then‑chord" figure. Once automatic, it frees your attention for the melody.
  3. Don't count rigidly. Once the notes are secure, let the phrases float. That space is the music.
  4. Use the speed control to find a tempo where every note is calm and even.
