The Easiest Mozart Pieces, Measured

Mozart is the composer beginners are told to start with, and the advice is half right. His mature piano sonatas are transparent in a way that punishes every mistake — nothing hides. But he also left behind a large body of very small keyboard music, written when he was a child himself, and that is genuinely where a beginner should begin.

We measured all 65 Mozart transcriptions in the library. They run from 1 out of 100 to 87. Here they are, easiest first.

An honest caveat before you scroll

The pieces at the very top of this list are short, sparse keyboard miniatures filed under Mozart's name, and you should know that music from this corner of his catalogue has a genuine attribution problem.

Wolfgang's earliest keyboard pieces come from the Nannerl Notenbuch — a teaching book his father Leopold began filling in 1759 and presented to Wolfgang's sister Maria Anna on her eighth name day. Leopold added to it until about 1764, and what went in was a mixture: anonymous minuets, pieces by copyists, works by Leopold himself, and music by other composers including C. P. E. Bach and Georg Christoph Wagenseil. Wolfgang's own first compositions are in there too — K. 1a–1f, then K. 2 to K. 5 — but they were largely written down in Leopold's hand, and scholars think Wolfgang himself notated only about four of them.

So when you see a very short, very easy "Mozart" keyboard piece, treat the name with a little caution. The one we can point at confidently on this list is the Minuet in F major, K. 2, which is one of the pieces from the Notenbuch attributed to Wolfgang. It measures 6 out of 100.

None of this changes what the music is like to play. It is a caveat about a label, not about a piece.

Why the early Mozart works so well for beginners

These pieces were written by, and for, small hands that had only just started. That is not a marketing story — it is literally what the Notenbuch was for.

The consequences are exactly what a learner wants. The range is narrow, so your hands stay in one place. The left hand is usually a simple broken chord or a held bass, so it can be learned in minutes and then forgotten about. The melodies are built from short phrases that repeat with small changes, so learning the first eight seconds gets you most of the next eight for free. And they end — after roughly a minute — which means you can finish one.

The ladder through Mozart

Under 10. The tiny early keyboard pieces, including K. 2. A first week's material. If you have never played anything, start here.

10–30. Short minuets and dances. Both hands are doing something, but never at the same speed.

30–60. The sonata movements most people can realistically reach, and the Lieder. This is where the transparency starts to matter: the notes are not many, but each one is exposed.

Above 60. The rest of the sonatas, the variation sets, the Rondo Alla Turca at speed. Mozart at this level is famously unforgiving — there is no thick texture to hide an uneven finger inside.

The trap in the middle

There is a specific way Mozart catches people out, and it is worth naming.

His music looks easy on the page and in letter notes, because there genuinely are not many notes. What is hard is evenness. A run of eight notes in the right hand has to sound like eight equal notes; in Chopin the pedal and the texture will cover a slightly lumpy one, and in Mozart nothing will.

That is why the speed control matters more here than almost anywhere else in the library. Take a Mozart passage down to half speed and listen specifically for whether the notes are the same length as each other — not whether you got them right. Getting them right comes quickly. Getting them even is the actual work, and it is also, when it lands, the thing that makes Mozart sound like Mozart.
