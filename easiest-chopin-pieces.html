The Easiest Chopin Pieces to Learn (and How to Start)

Let's be honest up front: most of Chopin is hard. He was a Polish composer (1810–1849) whose piano technique was without equal in his generation, and he wrote almost exclusively for the piano — often at a level that takes years to reach. (Wikipedia)

But there is a door in, and it's a beautiful one: a handful of his Preludes, Op. 28.

Where beginners should start: the Preludes

Chopin's Op. 28 is a set of 24 short preludes — some are ferocious, but several are short, slow and genuinely playable early on. These four are catalogued as easy in the DoReDog library:

  • Prelude Op. 28 No. 7 — the classic first Chopin. It's tiny, gentle, and one of the most rewarding "real Chopin" pieces a beginner can play.
  • Prelude Op. 28 No. 20 — a solemn chord procession. Almost no fast movement; the challenge is control and weight, not speed. Excellent for learning to play chords cleanly.
  • Prelude Op. 28 No. 4 — a slow, aching melody over quietly shifting chords. Famous, and far more approachable than its emotional depth suggests.
  • Prelude Op. 28 No. 6 — here the left hand carries the melody, which makes it a great exercise for a hand that usually just accompanies.

The realistic next step

Once those feel comfortable, the natural progression is the Nocturne Op. 9 No. 2 — often considered Chopin's most famous piece. Chopin wrote his three Op. 9 Nocturnes in 1831–32, when he was only about twenty. (Wikipedia)) It's rated medium here: the melody grows increasingly ornamented on each return, so it asks for patience — but it's within reach with slow practice.

The Raindrop Prelude (Op. 28 No. 15) is another good "next" target: it's the longest of the preludes, built around a repeated note many hear as falling rain.

What to leave for later

Be kind to yourself and postpone these: the Revolutionary Étude, the Fantaisie-Impromptu, and the Ballades. They're genuinely advanced, and attempting them too early is the fastest way to get discouraged.

How to practise Chopin as a beginner

  1. Slow it right down. Chopin's melodies need shape, and shape requires time.
  2. Hands separately first — his left-hand accompaniments often stretch further than they look. (See Hands‑Separate Practice.)
  3. Loop the ornaments. When the melody gets decorated, drill just that bar with the A–B loop.
  4. Let it breathe. Chopin without rubato sounds mechanical; once the notes are secure, ease off the strict counting.
