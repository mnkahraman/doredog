The Easiest Classical Pieces to Learn as a Beginner

The fastest way to fall in love with the piano is to play something beautiful early — before technique, before theory. The pieces below are famous, genuinely beginner‑friendly, and every one is written out in colour‑coded letter notes you can play in your browser right now. They're grouped from "play this first" to "your first taste of the great composers."

Start here — the most familiar, the most forgiving

Ode to Joy — Ludwig van Beethoven. The choral theme from the finale of Beethoven's Ninth Symphony. The melody moves almost entirely to neighbouring keys, so your hand barely has to move — which is exactly why it's the classic "first real piece."

Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star — W. A. Mozart. You already know how it goes, which does half the work for you. This is the nursery melody Mozart later turned into a celebrated set of variations. Simple, repetitive, and endlessly satisfying to get right.

Amazing Grace — Traditional. A slow, familiar hymn with a gentle, singable line — ideal for practising a smooth, connected melody without any rush.

Gentle and dreamy — where a slow tempo is the style

Gymnopédie No. 1 — Erik Satie. Satie's most famous piece is slow, spacious and hypnotic. Because it's meant to be unhurried, beginners aren't fighting the tempo — playing it slowly sounds correct.

Gymnopédie No. 2 and Gymnopédie No. 3 — Erik Satie. The two companions to No. 1, in the same calm, floating mood. Learn one and the others come easily.

Morning Mood — Edvard Grieg. The famous "sunrise" melody from Grieg's _Peer Gynt_. A flowing, gentle tune most people recognise instantly.

Written for learners — pieces composed to be first steps

Minuet in G — Christian Petzold. One of the most famous teaching pieces ever written. It comes from the 1725 _Notebook for Anna Magdalena Bach_ and was long credited to Bach himself, but is now attributed to the Dresden composer Christian Petzold. (Wikipedia)

The Happy Farmer — Robert Schumann. From Schumann's _Album for the Young_, a collection he wrote specifically for children learning the piano — so its pieces are short, cheerful and hands‑friendly by design.

Morning Prayer — P. I. Tchaikovsky. The opening piece of Tchaikovsky's _Album for the Young_ (Children's Album), another set written expressly for young learners. Calm, chordal and beautiful.

Bach's Little Preludes — short keyboard pieces Bach wrote as teaching material, perfect for a first taste of Baroque: Little Prelude in C (BWV 924), BWV 939, and the flowing Prelude in C minor (BWV 999).

Your first taste of the greats

Prelude in C (WTC I) — J. S. Bach. The serene opening of Bach's _Well‑Tempered Clavier_, Book I. It's built almost entirely from gently rolling broken chords in a steady pattern — hypnotic to play and far more approachable than its fame suggests.

Waltz Op. 39 No. 15 — Johannes Brahms. Brahms's beloved little A‑flat waltz — warm, lilting and a lovely first waltz to have under your fingers.

How to use this list

  1. Pick one that you already love the sound of. Motivation beats "correct order" every time.
  2. Open it and slow the tempo right down. Give yourself time to find each key.
  3. Right hand first, then left, then together — a bar at a time, looping anything tricky.
  4. Come back tomorrow. Ten minutes a day will carry you through this whole list faster than you'd believe.

Every piece here is free, in your browser, in letter notes — no sheet‑music reading required.
