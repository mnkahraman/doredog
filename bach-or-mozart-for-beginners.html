Bach or Mozart: Which Is Better for a Beginner?

Both names sound equally forbidding, and both wrote music specifically for people who could barely play. So we counted.

The medians are within one point of each other, which tells you almost nothing. The interesting number is the third row read two ways: Bach gives you more easy pieces, Mozart gives you a higher proportion of them.

The answer is Bach, and here is the specific reason

Not because his music is easier — the medians say it is not. Because Bach wrote teaching material on purpose, in a graded sequence, and it survived intact.

The Notebook for Anna Magdalena Bach is a household album of short keyboard pieces, and it is still the single best on-ramp to real music that exists. The minuets in it are thirty seconds long, sit in one hand position, and sound like finished pieces rather than exercises. Two of the most famous entries were not even written by Bach — the Minuets in G major and G minor are by Christian Petzold, which the library credits properly — but the collection is his, and it works.

Mozart wrote no equivalent. His easiest measured pieces are the Twelve Duos for two horns, K. 487 — scoring as low as 1 — and they are not piano music at all. They are two horn lines that happen to transcribe well. Charming, playable, and no path to anything.

Where Mozart wins

Once you are past the beginner stage, Mozart's proportion advantage becomes real. A third of his output here scores under 30, and the pieces in that band are actual piano writing — sonata movements, variations, dances — not transcriptions of something else. If you have six months behind you, Mozart offers more that you can play and that is idiomatic for the instrument.

Bach's easy pieces run out faster than they look like they will. Of his 359 pieces here, 305 score 30 or above, and the jump from the Anna Magdalena minuets to the Two-Part Inventions is a bigger step than beginners expect.

The order we would suggest

  1. The Anna Magdalena minuets — Menuet in G major scores 11
  2. Prelude in C, the first prelude of the Well-Tempered Clavier — scores 30, and is one pattern repeated
  3. Mozart's easier sonata movements and the Twinkle variations
  4. The Two-Part Inventions, when the first three feel comfortable
